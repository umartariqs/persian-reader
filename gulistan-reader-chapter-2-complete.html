Several travellers had agreed to journey together and to share hardship and ease. I wished to accompany them, but they would not consent. I said, ‘It is strange conduct from the generosity of great men to turn away from the companionship of the poor and withhold benefit and blessing. One of them said, ‘Do not be distressed by what you have heard. Recently a thief appeared in the guise of a dervish and enrolled himself among our company.’

.

Because the dervishes were themselves sound in character, suspicion of his meddlesome intent did not occur to them, and they accepted him as a companion.

The outward condition of the mystics is a patched cloak; that much is sufficient when you face the people.

Strive in action and wear whatever you wish: place a crown upon your head and carry a standard upon your shoulder.

[

Renunciation means abandoning the world, appetite, and caprice; devotion does not consist merely in abandoning fine clothes.

A man must remain a man even inside a padded coat; what use are weapons of war upon a coward?

.

One day we had travelled until nightfall and were sleeping at the foot of a fortress when the luckless thief picked up a companion’s ewer, pretending that he was going to purify himself, while in fact he was going to plunder.

Look at the ‘devout’ man who put on the dervish cloak, then made the Kaaba’s covering into a donkey’s blanket.

. . . .

Once he had disappeared from the dervishes’ sight, he climbed a tower and stole a casket. By the time daylight came, that dark-hearted man had travelled some distance, while his innocent companions slept. In the morning they were all taken into the fortress, beaten, and imprisoned. From that date we renounced companionship and adopted the path of solitude.

Safety lies in solitude.

When one member of a group acts ignorantly, neither the lesser nor the greater retains his standing.

Have you heard how one cow in a pasture can befoul all the cattle of a village?

: . .

I said, ‘Praise and gratitude belong to God, mighty and majestic, that I did not remain deprived of the dervishes’ blessing, although outwardly I fell away from companionship and became solitary. I have profited from the story you told, and this counsel will serve people like me throughout our lives.’

One uncouth person in an assembly greatly troubles the hearts of many wise people.

If they fill a pool with rosewater and a dog falls into it, the dog turns it into a muddy cesspool.

Once, in the congregational mosque of Baalbek, I was delivering words of admonition to a dispirited congregation, dead at heart, who had not travelled from the world of outward form to the world of meaning.

.

I saw that my breath was making no impression and my fire had no effect upon their wet firewood.

. .

I regretted trying to educate beasts and holding up a mirror in the quarter of the blind. Nevertheless, the door of meaning stood open and the chain of discourse grew long.

: « ». :

While explaining the meaning of the verse, ‘We are nearer to him than his jugular vein,’ I carried the discourse to the point where I said:

The Friend is nearer to me than I am to myself—and here is the difficulty: I am far from Him.

:

What can I do, and to whom can this be said? He is beside me, while I remain separated.

.

I was intoxicated by the wine of these words, with the dregs of the cup still in my hand, when a traveller passed along the edge of the gathering. The final round took effect upon him, and he gave a cry; the others joined him in uproar, and the unripe members of the assembly began to boil.

: ! !

I said, ‘Glory be to God! Those at a distance who possess awareness are present, while the sightless people nearby are far away.’

When the listener cannot understand the discourse, do not seek force of genius from the speaker.

Bring the wide playing-field of receptiveness, so that the eloquent man may strike the ball.

However often my venerable teacher Abu al-Faraj ibn al-Jawzi—may God have mercy upon him—commanded me to abandon musical audition and directed me toward seclusion and solitude, the vigour of youth prevailed in me and desire and caprice continued to seek their object.

:

I would inevitably take a step contrary to my teacher’s judgement and derive some pleasure from music and company. Whenever I remembered my sheikh’s advice, I would say:

If the judge sits with us, he will wave his hands in the dance; if the moral inspector drinks wine, he will excuse the drunkard.

Then one night I reached a gathering in which I saw a musician.

You would say his discordant stroke was tearing the vein of life; his voice was more unpleasant than news of a father’s death.

!

At times the companions had their fingers in their ears because of him, and at times upon their lips, signalling, ‘Silence!’

We rush toward the sound of songs because of their sweetness—but you are a singer whose silence would make us feel well.

No one finds pleasure in your performance except at the moment you stop and draw breath.

:

When that lute-player began to sing, I said to the master of the house, ‘For God’s sake—’

‘Pour mercury into my ears so that I cannot hear, or open the door for me so that I may leave.’

.

In short, out of consideration for my friends’ feelings I went along with them, and through many struggles brought that night to morning.

The muezzin raised his untimely call; he does not know how much of the night has passed.

Ask my eyelashes about the night’s length, for not a moment of sleep has entered my eyes.

.

In the morning, for the sake of blessing, I removed a turban from my head and a dinar from my belt, placed them before the singer, embraced him, and thanked him profusely.

.

My friends saw that my devotion toward him was contrary to my usual conduct and attributed it to feebleness of mind.

.

One of them began to object at length and reproached me: ‘You have acted in a manner unworthy of a wise man’s judgement. You gave the sheikhs’ cloak to a musician who in his whole life has never had a silver coin in his palm or a broken coin in his tambourine.’

A musician far from this blessed dwelling—no one has seen him twice in the same place.

The moment his voice rose straight from his mouth, the hair rose upon the people’s bodies.

The bird in the portico flew away in terror of him; he carried off our brains and tore open his own throat.

: .

I said, ‘It would be prudent for you to shorten your objections, for this man’s miraculous grace has become apparent to me.’

:

He said, ‘Will you not acquaint me with its nature, so that I too may draw near to him and ask forgiveness for the jokes I have made?’

: ! . !

I replied, ‘Certainly. My venerable sheikh repeatedly commanded me to abandon musical audition and delivered eloquent admonitions, but they never entered my receptive ear. Tonight my fortunate star and auspicious destiny guided me to this place, so that through this man’s agency I have repented: for the rest of my life I shall never again approach musical audition or social mixing.’

A beautiful voice from a palate, mouth, and sweet lips captivates the heart whether it sings or not.

But even if the mode is Oshshaq, Khorasan, or Hijaz, it does not become the throat of a repellent singer.

: ! . .

He replied, ‘Dear friend, offer me condolences, for this is no occasion for congratulations. When you last saw me, I worried about a loaf of bread; today I bear the anxieties of a whole world.’

If we have no worldly goods, we suffer; and if we have them, we are bound by love of them.

No veil is more inwardly troubling than distress of mind—whether one possesses or does not.

If you desire wealth, seek nothing but contentment, for it is a pleasant fortune.

If a rich man pours gold into your lap, do not regard his reward as greater—

:

For I have often heard from the great: the poor man’s patience is better than the rich man’s generosity.

Though Bahram should roast a wild ass for you, it is not equal to a locust’s leg offered by an ant.
